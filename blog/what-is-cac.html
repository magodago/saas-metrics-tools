What Is CAC? Customer Acquisition Cost Explained

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) is one of the most important SaaS metrics. This guide explains what it is, how to calculate it, and how to use it to grow profitably.

Definition of CAC

CAC stands for Customer Acquisition Cost. It is the total amount you spend on marketing and sales to acquire one new customer. In other words, it answers: “How much does it cost us to win one paying customer?”

For SaaS and subscription businesses, CAC is usually measured over a defined period (e.g. a month or a quarter). You add all costs that can be attributed to acquiring customers—ad spend, salaries for marketing and sales, tools, content, events—and divide by the number of new customers you acquired in that same period.

The CAC Formula

The formula is simple:

CAC = Total cost of acquisition ÷ Number of new customers

Total cost of acquisition includes all marketing and sales expenses: paid ads, content marketing, SEO, salaries (or allocated portion) for marketing and sales teams, CRM and marketing software, and any other spend that directly supports acquiring customers. Do not include product development or general overhead unless you have a clear allocation method.

New customers are typically counted when they make their first payment or sign a contract, depending on how your business defines “customer.” Consistency in definition is key so you can compare CAC over time.

Why CAC Matters for SaaS

CAC tells you how efficient your go-to-market motion is. A high CAC means you are spending a lot to win each customer; a lower CAC means you are acquiring customers more efficiently. For subscription businesses, the goal is to keep CAC low enough that the revenue from each customer (LTV) exceeds it by a healthy margin—typically at least 3:1 LTV to CAC.

Unit economics

CAC is a core input to unit economics. If your CAC is higher than the revenue (or gross margin) you get from a customer in the first month or year, you are losing money on each acquisition. That can be acceptable in early growth if you have funding and a path to improve LTV or reduce CAC, but it is not sustainable indefinitely.

Scaling

Understanding CAC helps you decide when and how to scale. If CAC stays stable or decreases as you spend more, you can invest more in acquisition. If CAC increases as you scale (e.g. because you exhaust the easiest channels), you need to improve conversion, retention, or find new channels before pouring more budget.

How to Calculate CAC Correctly

To calculate CAC correctly, use a consistent time window (e.g. last 30 or 90 days) and include all relevant costs. Count only new customers acquired in that same window. What to include in “total cost”

Include: paid advertising (Google, Facebook, etc.), content and SEO (if you allocate cost), marketing and sales team salaries (or the portion of time spent on acquisition), marketing and sales tools (CRM, email, ads platforms), events, affiliate or partner payouts, and any other direct acquisition spend. Exclude: product development, customer success (post-sale), and general admin unless you have a clear allocation.

Benchmarks and What’s “Good”

There is no single “good” CAC—it depends on your LTV, margin, and growth stage. A common rule of thumb is that LTV should be at least 3 times CAC. So if your CAC is $500, you want LTV of $1,500 or more. Early-stage B2B SaaS often has higher CAC ($500–$2,000+); SMB or self-serve products may have much lower CAC ($50–$200).

You can compare your CAC to industry benchmarks (e.g. from reports by OpenView, ChartMogul, or similar) but always interpret benchmarks in the context of your own LTV and payback period.

How to Improve CAC

Improving CAC means either spending less to acquire the same number of customers or acquiring more customers with the same spend. Tactics include: improving conversion rates on landing pages and in the funnel, refining targeting so you attract higher-intent users, testing cheaper channels (content, SEO, community), improving sales efficiency (shorter cycles, higher win rates), and increasing retention so that each acquired customer contributes more over time (effectively improving LTV:CAC).

Common Mistakes

Common mistakes include: excluding key costs (e.g. salaries or tools), using revenue instead of cost, mixing time periods (e.g. annual cost with monthly new customers), and counting sign-ups or trials instead of paying customers. Avoid these by defining CAC once, documenting what you include, and sticking to it.
